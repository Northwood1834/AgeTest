# Session timing and evidence boundary

## Product rule

A session contains exactly 12 short tasks (normally about 4–6 minutes). The next session unlocks 20 hours after completion. This produces a practical once-per-day rhythm without a midnight reset, asks for a modest period of sustained focus, and prevents continuous farming.

The 20-hour value is an evidence-informed product choice, **not** a medically validated optimal interval. Published cognitive-training protocols are heterogeneous, and current evidence does not establish an exact cooldown measured in hours.

## Evidence considered

- Lampit A, Hallock H, Valenzuela M. “Computerized Cognitive Training in Cognitively Healthy Older Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Effect Modifiers.” *PLoS Medicine* 11(11), 2014. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pmed.1001756](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001756). The review found modest average effects and substantial dependence on study/design features; it does not validate a consumer-game dose or exact hourly interval.
- Tullo D, Feng Y, Pahor A, Cote JM, Seitz AR, Jaeggi SM. “Investigating the Role of Individual Differences in Adherence to Cognitive Training.” *Journal of Cognition* 6(1), 2023. DOI: [10.5334/joc.315](https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.315). Its literature review and large adherence analysis describe mixed findings for spacing and note that existing inter-session ranges are narrow.
- Wang Z et al. “Training Distribution as a Key Manipulator for Spatial Updating Transfer.” *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 8:217, 2014. DOI: [10.3389/fnhum.2014.00217](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00217). It supports investigating distributed rather than massed practice but does not establish a universal once-per-day rule.

## Interpretation

The app uses a short fixed volume to reduce fatigue, keeps the set long enough to require several minutes of continuous task switching, and uses a long cooldown to enforce distributed play. It does **not** claim that this schedule improves cognition, prevents decline, treats disease, or matches a clinical training program. The exact limit can be revised if direct dose-response evidence for comparable short, unsupervised, mixed-task sessions becomes available; a change must be reported as a product-rule change, not as newly proven medicine.
